## Mixing paint in public

Alice and Bob want a shared secret, but every message between them is read by **Eve**.
Start with paint.

1. They agree in public on a **common colour**, yellow.
2. Each picks a **secret colour** and tells no one: red for Alice, blue for Bob.
3. Each mixes the common colour with their secret one and sends the mixture across in
   the open.
4. Each adds their own secret colour to the mixture they received.

Both final pots hold one part yellow, one part red and one part blue, so they are the
same colour. Eve saw yellow and both mixtures, but mixing those gives _two_ parts yellow.
To match, she would have to pour a colour back out.

The analogy has a limit, and the last step says where: paint mixing is an average, and an
average can be undone. The real thing needs an operation that can't.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="exchange">

## The same thing with numbers

Swap paint for arithmetic mod a prime. The public colour becomes two public numbers:
the prime **p = 23** and the generator **g = 2**. Here p = 2 × 11 + 1 is a _safe prime_,
and the powers of 2 mod 23 cycle through 11 values, listed under the first step.

1. Alice picks a private **a = 6**, Bob a private **b = 8**. In this module they come
   from a seeded, non-cryptographic generator, so they are for display only.
2. Alice computes **A = 2⁶ mod 23 = 18** and Bob **B = 2⁸ mod 23 = 3**, by
   square-and-multiply, one exponent bit at a time.
3. They send A and B in the open. Each checks that what arrived is in the subgroup
   (**y¹¹ mod 23 = 1**).
4. Alice computes **Bᵃ = 3⁶ mod 23 = 16**; Bob computes **Aᵇ = 18⁸ mod 23 = 16**.

Both get **16**, because (gᵇ)ᵃ = gᵃᵇ = (gᵃ)ᵇ. The number 16 never crossed the channel.
Turn on **Eve’s view** to see what an eavesdropper has: p, g, A and B, and nothing
else.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="eve">

## What Eve can do

Eve has **p = 23, g = 2, A = 18 and B = 3**. To get the secret she needs a, and getting
a from A = gᵃ mod p is the **discrete logarithm** problem. With p = 23 she just tries
every exponent: 2¹, 2², 2³, … until one gives 18. After six guesses she has **a = 6**,
and **B⁶ mod 23 = 16**, the secret. Her own lane fills in as she works it out.

That only works because p is tiny. The table at the end shows how the number of guesses
grows: roughly ten times per extra digit of p. Real groups (RFC 3526, RFC 7919) use a
617-digit p, and the worst case becomes a 617-digit number of guesses. Better attacks
than guessing exist, and 2048 bits is chosen so that they are out of reach too.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="mitm">

## Mallory in the middle

Eve only listens. **Mallory** can also change what's sent. Plain Diffie-Hellman never
says _who_ sent a share, so Mallory, with p = 467, catches Alice's A and Bob's B and
forwards shares of her own.

Alice ends up with a secret she shares with Mallory, Bob with a different one, and
Mallory holds both. When Alice sends a message (here 42, with a toy cipher), Mallory
decrypts it, reads it, re-encrypts it for Bob, and Bob sees exactly what he expected.
The four lanes show who holds what.

The fix is to **prove who sent each share**. If Bob signs B with his private key, Alice
can check it with his public key, and Mallory can't forge that signature. That is
[RSA signing, from module 5](/rsa). And it is what [TLS 1.3](/#module-tls) does: the
server signs the whole handshake, key shares included, with a key its certificate
vouches for.

</Chapter>
