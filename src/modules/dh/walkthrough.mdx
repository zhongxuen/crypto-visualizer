<Chapter id="paint">

## Mixing paint in public

Alice and Bob want a shared secret, but every message between them is read by **Eve**.
Start with paint.

<Phase id="pots">

They agree in public on a **common colour**, yellow. Each also picks a **secret
colour** and tells no one: red for Alice, blue for Bob.

</Phase>

<Phase id="mix">

Each mixes the common colour with their own secret, averaging in <Term id="oklab" />.
That looks like real paint, and you can't see by eye which secret went in.

</Phase>

<Phase id="swap">

They send the mixtures across in the open. Eve sees both pots go past.

</Phase>

<Phase id="mix-again">

Each adds their own secret colour to the mixture they received. Both final pots hold
one part yellow, one part red and one part blue, so they are the same colour.

</Phase>

<Phase id="eve">

Eve saw yellow and both mixtures, but mixing those gives _two_ parts yellow. To match,
she would have to pour a colour back out. That is also where the analogy stops: paint
mixing is an average, and an average can be undone. The real thing needs an operation
that can't.

</Phase>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="exchange">

## The same thing with numbers

Swap paint for arithmetic <Term id="mod" /> a prime: numbers that wrap round like a
clock.

<Phase id="params">

The common colour becomes two public numbers: the prime **p = 23** and the generator
**g = 2**. Here p = 2 × 11 + 1 is a _safe prime_, and the powers of 2 mod 23 cycle
through 11 values.

</Phase>

<Phase id="private">

Alice picks a private **a = 6**, Bob a private **b = 8**. In this module they come from
a seeded, non-cryptographic generator, so they are for display only.

</Phase>

<Phase id="alice-public">

Alice computes **A = 2⁶ mod 23 = 18** by square-and-multiply: one exponent bit at a
time, square, and multiply by g when the bit is 1. The clock shows the running value.

</Phase>

<Phase id="bob-public">

Bob does the same with his b: **B = 2⁸ mod 23 = 3**.

</Phase>

<Phase id="exchange">

They send A and B in the open. Each checks that what arrived is in the subgroup
(**y¹¹ mod 23 = 1**). Turn on **Eve’s view** to see what an eavesdropper has: p, g, A
and B, and nothing else.

</Phase>

<Phase id="shared">

Alice computes **Bᵃ = 3⁶ mod 23 = 16**; Bob computes **Aᵇ = 18⁸ mod 23 = 16**. Both get
**16**, because (gᵇ)ᵃ = gᵃᵇ = (gᵃ)ᵇ. The number 16 never crossed the channel.

</Phase>

<Phase id="practice">

Real groups use the same steps with a 617-digit p, and most TLS today uses X25519, the
same idea on an elliptic curve.

</Phase>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="eve">

## What Eve can do

<Phase id="view">

Eve has **p = 23, g = 2, A = 18 and B = 3**. To get the secret she needs a, and getting
a from A = gᵃ mod p is the **discrete logarithm** problem.

</Phase>

<Phase id="search">

With p = 23 she just tries every exponent: 2¹, 2², 2³, … until one gives 18. Each guess
is the last one times g, so the clock steps round.

</Phase>

<Phase id="found">

After six guesses she has **a = 6**, and **B⁶ mod 23 = 16**, the secret. Her own lane
fills in as she works it out.

</Phase>

<Phase id="growth">

That only works because p is tiny. The number of guesses grows roughly ten times per
extra digit of p. Real groups (RFC 3526, RFC 7919) use a 617-digit p, and the worst case
becomes a 617-digit number of guesses. Better attacks than guessing exist, and 2048 bits
is chosen so that they are out of reach too.

</Phase>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="mitm">

## Mallory in the middle

<Phase id="setup">

Eve only listens. **Mallory** can also change what's sent. She sits on the channel with
p = 467 and two private keys of her own.

</Phase>

<Phase id="swap">

Plain Diffie-Hellman never says _who_ sent a share, so Mallory catches Alice's A and
Bob's B and forwards shares of her own. The channel is now two channels, with her in
the gap.

</Phase>

<Phase id="secrets">

Alice ends up with a secret she shares with Mallory (●), Bob with a different one (■),
and Mallory holds both.

</Phase>

<Phase id="relay">

When Alice sends a message (here 42, with a toy cipher), Mallory decrypts it, reads it,
re-encrypts it for Bob, and Bob sees exactly what he expected.

</Phase>

<Phase id="fix">

The fix is to **prove who sent each share**. If Bob signs B with his private key, Alice
can check it with his public key, and Mallory can't forge that signature. That is
[RSA signing, from module 5](/rsa). And it is what [TLS 1.3](/#module-tls) does: the
server signs the whole handshake, key shares included, with a key its certificate
vouches for.

</Phase>

</Chapter>
